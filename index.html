Light Pollution: Losing the Night

Artificial light at night changes what people can see, when animals behave, and how urban environments shape the night. This visualisation follows the story from global sky visibility, to Australian light exposure, to wildlife and human sleep patterns.

1. The night sky is not equally dark everywhere

Light pollution can be seen through reduced night-sky visibility. Lower limiting magnitude means fewer stars are visible, while higher values indicate darker skies. The first visuals show global observations and an Australian luminance-style heatmap.

Limiting magnitude scale: used by both top visualisations. Lower values mean fewer visible stars and brighter, more light-polluted skies.

2. Wildlife interacts with the night, not just the land

Artificial light can affect animals by changing the timing, direction, or safety of night-time behaviour. The penguin visualisation focuses on arrival timing after sunset, while the turtle graph uses city proximity as a proxy for likely urban light exposure.

3. Outdoor lighting is infrastructure, not just brightness

The effects of artificial light depend on where lights are placed, what technology they use, and how bright they are. The streetlight graphs use a reduced Western Power dataset so the page loads quickly while preserving bulb type and wattage.

4. The urban night also affects people

This section shifts from animals to people. It compares sleep timing in Major Cities and Regional/Remote Australia. It does not prove light pollution causes later sleep, but it shows that urban and regional night-time patterns differ.

What this shows: sleep and wake times differ between Major Cities and Regional/Remote Australia across age groups. Source placeholder: Australian sleep statistics, sleep_stats.csv.

5. What the data suggests

Together, the visuals show that artificial light is measurable, spatially concentrated, and relevant to both wildlife and people. The project does not claim direct causation for every pattern, but it highlights where light exposure, animal behaviour, lighting infrastructure and sleep timing intersect.

Main argument: light pollution is not just an astronomy issue. It is an environmental and public-health issue shaped by urban infrastructure, coastal exposure, lighting technology, and the loss of natural darkness.
