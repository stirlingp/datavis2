Light Pollution: worth losing sleep?

Unneeded artificial light at night-time has adverse effects for humans and animals alike. This visualisation explores global sky visibility, to Australian light exposure, to wildlife and human sleep patterns.

FIT2179 Data Visualisation 2 Stirling Perrott — 34968253 May 2026

1. The night sky isn't entirely dark

Light pollution can be seen through reduced night-sky visibility. By Astronomy conventions, a higher magnitude represents a dimmer star, implying that low limiting magnitudes lower the number of stars we can see. The first visuals show global observations and an Australian luminance-style heatmap.

What this shows: light pollution is a widespread and global problem, with obvious brighter patches near major cities. Source: Globe at Night maps and data, 2025 observation export.

2. Wildlife interacts with the night, not just the land

Artificial light can affect animals by changing the timing, direction, or safety of night-time behaviour. Since many animals rely on the natural light cues to navigate, when the light is bright enough the animals’ natural patterns can be disrupted.

Penguin arrival timing after sunset

Nightly arrival timing compared with sunset and cloud cover.

What this shows: animal behaviour is linked to night-time conditions, with arrival timing compared against sunset and cloud cover. Source: Phillip Island Nature Parks / penguins.org.au dataset used for nightly penguin arrival visualisation.

Turtle records near urban light sources

Cumulative proximity of Flatback Turtle records to major coastal cities.

What this shows: Turtles are famously affected by city light pollution as the hatched babies often wander towards city lights instead of into the water. This in conjunction with the large percentage of turtles living near cities poses a significant risk to their survival. Source: Atlas of Living Australia occurrence records for Flatback Turtle.

3. Outdoor lighting is infrastructure, not just brightness

The effects of artificial light depend on where lights are placed, what technology they use, and how bright they are.

4. The urban night also affects people

Light pollution also has adverse effects for humans. This section compares sleep timing in Major Cities and Regional/Remote Australia. Light pollution is especially prevalent around cities and studies have shown that sleep timing and quality are worse in areas of high light pollution.

Sleep timing in major cities and regional Australia

Dumbbell chart comparing bedtime and wake time across age groups.

What this shows: sleep and wake times differ between Major Cities and Regional/Remote Australia across age groups. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics measured physical activity and sleep data.

5. What the data shows

Together, the visuals show that artificial light is measurable, spatially concentrated, and relevant to both wildlife and people. Clearly greater steps must be taken to reduce the effects of light pollution in Australia and additionally at a global scale.

Main argument: light pollution is not just an astronomy issue. It is an environmental and public-health issue shaped by urban infrastructure, coastal exposure, lighting technology, and the loss of natural darkness.
